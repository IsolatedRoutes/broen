# Card rules (written as AI instructions)

CARD RULES (the owner's final rules for the explanation and the card; where anything above conflicts with them, these win). 'Translation' means the English meaning given after 'means'; 'the card' means everything the learner sees: the sentence box, the forms list, the explanation and the related words. OPENING: the first sentence gives only the Danish word and its English meaning: "X" means "Y". ALLOWED ADDITIONS: after the opening, every sentence must be exactly one of these: how the word is built, or which word it comes from (said once; "vækst" is the noun for the verb "at vokse" (to grow)); a fact about the thing that is specific to Denmark and helps the learner understand the word in Danish daily life (how an institution, custom, rule or habit the word names works; never trivia such as famous landmarks, brands, records, dates or history); a mix-up with one named similar word, saying what each means, each with its own example; a different meaning or use of this same word, with its own example; a spelling or form that English speakers would get wrong; the politeness level, when the word is blunt, rude or formal; the moment you use it, in plain words, with a short quoted exchange ("Te eller kaffe?" "Det er lige meget." (Tea or coffee? It does not matter.)); a literal translation after the real meaning ("literally, ..."); the small word this word must be used with, with an example; a very common phrase or word partner the learner will meet, with its own example ("Prøv at genstarte routeren" (Try restarting the router)); what a part of the word means in other words it appears in, with examples (lige in "lige nu" (right now) and "lige meget" (it does not matter)). If a sentence is none of these, delete it. THE OPENING ALONE IS NOT ENOUGH: always add at least one allowed addition, the most useful one available (usually a common phrase or word partner, such as "et bundt tulipaner" (a bunch of tulips) for a plain flower, or what a part of the word means elsewhere), because a bare translation teaches nothing the forms list does not already show. DO NOT REPEAT THE TRANSLATION: never describe the thing in English words the translation already gives (not "a gift you give at Christmas" after "a Christmas present"). Exception: you may describe a thing that is specific to Denmark. SAY WHICH MEANING ONLY WHEN ENGLISH HAS SEVERAL: if the English translation has more than one meaning, name the one meant in a few words ("as in the body part"). If the English word has one meaning, add nothing (not "the one in your mouth" for tongue). NO REPETITION: every sentence states one idea that nothing else on the card already says. A sentence that repeats the meaning, a form, a use already given, or an example with the same sentence pattern as another example on the card is not allowed, even when its type is allowed above. Each type of addition appears at most once, except different uses of the word, which each get their own example. Exception: two examples that differ in one word on purpose to show a difference ("Hvem kender dig?" (Who knows you?) and "Hvem kender du?" (Whom do you know?)) count as one example. NO CONTRADICTIONS: the explanation must agree with the card. If it names a form different from the one the card shows (card: "en forkølelse", explanation: "Jeg er forkølet"), give each form its own example and say when to use which; otherwise leave it out. ONE ENGLISH WORDING: the translation in the opening uses the same English words as the first line of the forms list, written as natural English: if that line lists several meanings separated by a slash, join them with "or" (not a slash); if that line is a whole sentence or has extra words, use the English word or phrase that matches the Danish. STAY ON THE CARD'S WORD: mention another Danish word only to prevent a mix-up or to show how the word is built; never drift to a word that only shares a part with it (not "dobbeltværelse" on "venteværelse"). LEAD-IN FOR EVERY EXAMPLE: an example after the opening always says what it is an example of. Use one of two forms. (1) To show what the word or phrase means, or an extra meaning, partner or form it has, put "For example," in front of it ("It also describes water turning to ice. For example, "Vandet fryser" (The water freezes)."); a second example of that kind starts "For another example,". (2) To show when to use it, name the situation first ("To say you are finished, use "Jeg er færdig" (I am finished)." or "When you are cold, you say: "Jeg fryser" (I am cold)."). Never put an example straight after a colon that follows an explanation (not "It also describes water turning to ice: "Vandet fryser"", not "X is also a bedroom: Y"). EN AND ET: a word with no article still has a gender, shown by its 'the' form (legetøjet is an et-word, morgenmaden an en-word). Name only the article that matches the word, never 'en and et'; if you are not certain of the gender, write 'without an article' and name neither en nor et: This word has no article, so leave out "et": "Jeg har legetøj" (I have toys). The example must show the same thing the sentence says, and to point to one thing of that kind say: When you talk about one specific kind of toy, use its name: "en bamse" (a teddy bear). RELATED WORDS: exactly 3 or 4. Each is from the same word family, a real mix-up, a true opposite or synonym, or a member of a small set. A word that only shares a topic is not allowed (not mund or tand for tunge, not klinik for venteværelse). None may repeat anything in the explanation or be part of the card's own phrase.

WRITING RULES (the owner's final rules for every explanation the AI writes, for words, grammar points and chat answers alike). CLEAR LANGUAGE: write every sentence for a reader who has only this card and cannot ask anyone. (1) Introduce each meaning or use with a full sentence that has a subject and a verb and names the situation the learner is in, in actions they can picture (count, point to, ask, say, write, buy, describe): "When you count in order (first, second, third), it means "second"" and "When you point to the other one of two things, it means "other"". A label fragment such as "For order" or "For the remaining one of two" is not a sentence the learner can use. (2) Put one tiny example right after the situation, with its English in brackets. (3) When you refer back to a meaning, repeat it in quotes ("the second car" or "the other car"); do not point at it with a word such as either, both, this, that, the latter or the former. (4) Choose the simplest correct word for each idea; a plain grammar word (verb, noun, adjective, adverb, pronoun, preposition, article, singular, plural, past tense) is fine when it is shorter and clearer than a description, with a tiny example in the same sentence. (5) Before you finish, read each sentence as someone who has never seen the card: if they would ask "what do you mean?", add the missing noun, action or example. NATURAL LANGUAGE: the Danish must be what a Dane would really say, and the English must be what an English speaker would really say, never a word-for-word copy. When an example shows a difference that English has too (who and whom), use the English form people really say there ("To whom are you speaking?"), not a stiff one ("Whom do you know?"). EXAMPLE MATCHES THE STATEMENT: an example must show exactly what the sentence before it says: the same article, the same number (singular or plural) and the same use. A statement about leaving out "et" is not followed by an example that only works because it is plural; the sentence must make clear which meaning it covers. EXACT NOUNS: name the exact thing. Never write 'it', 'one' or 'its name' when the reader cannot tell what it points at; use the specific noun (say the type of toy, not its name). SHORT SENTENCES: one idea per sentence, about 20 words at most. CERTAIN CLAIMS ONLY: write a claim about usage or culture only if it is certainly true of everyday Danish. Do not write 'often', 'usually' or 'more often' unless you are sure; otherwise leave the claim out.
